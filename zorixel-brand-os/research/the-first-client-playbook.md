PHASE 01 · CHOOSE YOUR NICHE
One niche. Picked on purpose.
The single fastest way to stall is to stay a generalist who helps "local businesses." You want to be the
person who helps garage door companies, or septic pumpers, or tree services. Specific is easier to
sell, easier to refer, and easier to get good at.
Why one niche beats many
When you pick one niche, three things get easier at once. Your outreach message gets sharper, because
you know exactly what this kind of owner struggles with. Your delivery gets faster, because every client
needs the same setup. And your reputation compounds, because the third garage-door client is far easier
to land once you can say you already work with two others just like them.
Mixing niches does the opposite. Every message starts from scratch, every setup feels custom, and you
never build the momentum of being known for one thing.
THE FILTER
A good starter niche is local, phone-first, and emergency-or-urgency driven, where one job is worth
real money and reviews visibly decide who gets the call. The thirty niches in Phase 11 all pass this
test. Pick whichever you have some connection to, or simply whichever you find least boring.
A word on "boring"
Nobody dreams of running a grease-trap-cleaning marketing agency. That's the point. The boring niches
are wide open precisely because they're boring. The owners are out on the job missing calls, and almost
no agency is competing for them. Boring is where the money is least defended.
And remember what you're building here. Not a sprawling agency with a team and an office, just a one-
person operation where you pick a lane, get good at one simple service, and own it. The narrower the
niche, the easier that is.
FOR THE REST OF THIS PLAYBOOK
We'll use appliance repair as our example from here on. Pick yours now, before you turn the page,
and read every example as if it said your niche instead. The 30-niche list is in Phase 11 if you want to
choose before continuing.

PHASE 03 · AUDIT THE PROSPECT
Walk in knowing the one thing.
Once you've got a town with the right shape, pick businesses from the tail and run each through a
quick audit. You're not building a case file. You're finding the one thing worth mentioning in your first
message. Find the gap first, then reach out.
Open the listing and look for three things, in order. First, the review count against the competitors right
above them. That's almost always your lead. Second, whether the recent reviews dried up. Third, whether
there's even a website linked. You can spot all three in about fifteen seconds once you've done it a few
times.
RECENCY COUNTS AS MUCH AS THE NUMBER
A shop with forty reviews from the last few months is in better shape than one with two hundred
that stopped two years ago. Google rewards recency. If the recent reviews dried up, that's part of
your angle too.
The audit, one per business
A · THE REVIEW GAP (YOUR PRIMARY ANGLE)
This shop's review count vs. the top competitor's count. How recent is their last review?
B · LISTING COMPLETENESS
Photos present? Description filled in? Hours listed? Owner replies to reviews? Website linked?
C · WEBSITE STATUS
No website? Dated or poor on mobile? No clear way to call or book? Or solid, no issue?
D · YOUR ONE LEAD-IN
Big review gap, stale reviews, or no/dated website. Pick one. That's the line you'll open with.
SAVED FOR LATER
Most of these niches are phone-first, and the owners miss calls while they're under a machine or up
a ladder. That's a real problem worth solving, but it's the second conversation, not the first. Lead
with reviews. Offer call coverage once you've delivered.

PHASE 04 · REACH OUT
You don't write it. You send it.
Most outreach fails because it leads with the sender. Yours leads with their business. You've done
thirty seconds of homework, you mention one specific thing, and you make the ask tiny: a yes-or-no
question, not a request to block out half an hour. That alone puts you ahead of almost everyone else in
their inbox.
MESSAGE 1 · THE OPENER (EMAIL OR DM)
Hey [name], I was looking at [business] and noticed you've got some genuinely great reviews. Nice
work. The thing is, [top competitor] is showing up above you mostly because they've got more of them. I
put together a quick, simple way to close that gap that's been getting my clients fifteen-plus new five-
star reviews a week. Want me to send it over? T akes about thirty seconds to look at. 
Why it works: "I was looking at [business]" proves it isn't a mass blast. Naming the competitor creates quiet urgency.
Nobody likes being out-ranked. And the ask is a tiny yes-or-no, so saying yes is the path of least resistance.
The follow-up is where the money is
Most replies don't come on the first message. They come on the second or third. So the follow-up isn't
optional. It's the job. Three messages across five days. Don't re-pitch. Just resurface.
MESSAGE 2 · THE SOFT BUMP (+24 HOURS)
Hey [name], salespeople are the worst, so I get it if you'd like me to stop bugging you. That said, I'd hate
for you to miss out on closing the review gap on [competitor] just because I didn't send one more note.
Alright if I send the video over? 
MESSAGE 3 · THE EASY OUT (+3 DAYS)
Sometimes it's just not meant to be, [name], and I get it. Before I close your file, I wanted to follow up one
last time to make sure you're absolutely, positively not interested. If you are, drop me a line. If I don't hear
back, I'll leave you to it and keep up the great work. 
THE FOLLOW-UP RULE
Giving them an easy way out signals you're not desperate, which is often exactly what gets the
reply. The follow-up isn't persuasion. It's just visibility. Inside HighLevel, all three of these send
automatically once you add the prospect.

PHASE 07 · HANDLE THE OBJECTIONS
The same few questions, every time.
When you have a calm, honest answer ready, the objection stops being a wall and becomes the
moment they decide to trust you. Here's what's behind the common ones, and what to say.
Q"If it's that simple, why don't I just do it myself?"
They could, in theory. But after working with enough small businesses, here's the truth: they know they need reviews,
they've probably even tried, but they don't have a system. They're not going to build an automation or remember to
text after every job. They'd rather pay you to make it happen automatically than spend hours a month doing it
themselves. The best long-term clients are usually the ones who already tried and failed on their own.
Q"Will this annoy my customers?"
No, because the first message isn't even an ask, it's a check-in. The requests are spaced out and worded like a
person, not a robot. Most customers are glad to help when you make it a ten-second tap. And the reminder only goes
to people who haven't already left one, so nobody gets messaged twice.
Q"What if I don't have many customers yet?"
Even a few reviews a month compounds fast, and Google rewards recency. A steady trickle of fresh reviews beats a
big pile of old ones. The system makes sure every single job gets its shot at becoming a review, so nothing slips
through. Slow and steady still climbs the rankings.
Q"How do I know it's actually working?"
You'll both watch the same dashboard. Review count going up, responses going out, recent reviews stacking. And the
honest version of this answer is the strongest: if you don't see new reviews in the first 14 days, you don't pay. That
guarantee is easy to make because the system reliably delivers, and offering it removes the last bit of risk on their
side.
THE FRAME UNDERNEATH ALL OF IT
You're not selling them something they don't need. Their competitor is already winning calls that
should be theirs, just by looking better on Google. You're showing up with the fix to a problem
they're already bleeding from. That's not a pitch. It's a favor with a price on it.

PHASE 07 · YOUR OWN OBJECTIONS
And the ones you'll tell yourself.
The owner's objections are easy compared to the ones in your own head. Here are the four that stop

Q"I don't have any experience."
You don't need any. The niche is picked, the messages are written, the system clones in pre-built. The one thing this

You're not building software from scratch or learning to code. You're cloning a finished system and changing the

different than you expected, you can still deliver the result by hand while you learn.
Q"What if my niche is different?"
Local businesses may sell different things, but they share the same reputation problem. They need more trust, more
reviews, and more consistent follow-up. The system is identical whether you're working with appliance repair or
septic pumping. Only the words in the messages change.
Q"I don't have time for this."
The whole point of starting with one simple service is that it doesn't require much time. A few focused hours a week
works the plan. You're not building a complicated agency. You're installing one repeatable thing, again and again. The
complexity, and the hours, only come if you try to do everything at once. So don't.
The first client is rarely about the money. It's the proof.
Once you've landed one, the story in your head changes. It stops being "can I do this" and becomes "how many
more." That shift is worth more than the first $300.
